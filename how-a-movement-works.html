How a Mechanical Movement Works

Five systems work together inside every mechanical watch. Follow the energy from the crown to the hands and the whole movement starts to make sense.

  • Read6 min
  • RevisedNov 2026
  • LevelBeginner
FIG. 01POWER: MAINSPRING & BARRELSHEET 01

Power: mainspring & barrel

Winding the crown, or the swing of an automatic rotor, tightens the mainspring, a long coiled strip sealed inside a toothed drum called the barrel. As the spring relaxes it turns the barrel, slowly and with steady force. A fully wound spring commonly runs the watch for around 38 to 48 hours, which is the power reserve.

FIG. 02TRANSMISSION: THE GEAR TRAINSHEET 01

Transmission: the gear train

The barrel drives a train of wheels, each meshing with a smaller pinion on the next arbor. Every stage multiplies the speed.

  • The centre wheel turns once an hour and carries the minute hand.
  • The third wheel steps the speed up again.
  • The fourth wheel turns once a minute and often carries the seconds hand.
FIG. 03DISTRIBUTION: THE ESCAPEMENTSHEET 01

Distribution: the escapement

Left alone, the gear train would spin out in seconds. The escapement stops that. The escape wheel pushes against a two-armed pallet fork, which locks and releases it one tooth at a time. On each release the fork gives the balance a tiny push. This is the tick you hear.

FIG. 04REGULATION: BALANCE & HAIRSPRINGSHEET 01

Regulation: balance & hairspring

The balance wheel swings back and forth on a fine hairspring. Because each swing takes the same time, it acts as the watch's metronome. Most modern movements beat at 21,600 or 28,800 times an hour. Jewels, usually synthetic rubies, serve as low-friction bearings for the fastest-moving parts.

FIG. 05CONTROL: WINDING, SETTING & THE ROTORSHEET 01

Control: winding, setting & the rotor

The keyless works behind the crown let you wind in one position and set the hands in another. In an automatic, a half-moon rotor swings with every movement of your wrist and winds the mainspring through a set of reverser wheels. A slipping bridle stops it from being overwound.
